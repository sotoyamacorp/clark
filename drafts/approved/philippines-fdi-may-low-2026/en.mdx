# Philippine FDI falls to $210m in May, an 11-year low

## Introduction

Net foreign direct investment (FDI, short for Foreign Direct Investment) into the Philippines fell to $210 million in May 2026, its lowest monthly level in more than 11 years. Yet the Board of Investments (BOI) and the Philippine Economic Zone Authority (PEZA) reported rising approved investment over roughly the same period.

The difference is fundamental: BSP FDI is actual cross-border funding recorded on a net basis, while an approval is a future project plan. A company should not treat the two as interchangeable.

The 2021 spike mainly reflects a rebound as pandemic-depressed global investment activity resumed; since then, annual inflows have moved within roughly 80-90% of the prior year, without a clear recovery trend. At $7.79bn, 2025 was the second-lowest of the past six years, behind only 2020, when the pandemic froze most activity. Local media described the 2025 level as a "five-year low" (excluding pandemic-hit 2020, it is the weakest year since 2021). The weakness in January-May 2026 therefore looks less like a one-month blip and more like a continuation of the gradual decline that has been building since 2024.

## Is this Philippine-specific, or a regional trend?

To check whether the Philippine decline is an isolated case or part of a broader Southeast Asian slowdown, we compared 2025 figures for major ASEAN economies. Each country reports investment using a different methodology — government-approved plans, actually realized or disbursed capital, or net balance-of-payments flows — so the absolute figures below are not directly comparable. Read the direction of change rather than the size of the numbers.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Read the trend, not the scale</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Indonesia's and Malaysia's numbers look far larger, but that is because they use broader concepts — realized investment or approved investment — that can include domestically funded capital and reinvested earnings. That differs from BSP's narrower "net FDI" concept, which nets out purely cross-border funding. Measured on the same basis, the gap would likely be far smaller. Use this table to read direction, not magnitude.</p></div>

The table suggests Southeast Asia's FDI environment is not uniformly weakening. Vietnam's disbursed capital, driven by manufacturing and supply-chain relocation, is at a five-year high, and Malaysia's approved foreign investment has grown sharply on data-center and semiconductor-related projects. The Philippines stands out by contrast: even as BOI and PEZA approvals rise, actual net inflows on a BSP basis are shrinking — a pattern distinct from its ASEAN peers.

That points more toward Philippine-specific frictions — a relatively slow conversion from approval to actual disbursement, or a sharper pullback in intercompany debt flows — than toward a region-wide funding drought. Given the differing statistical standards across countries, however, this remains a reasonable interpretation rather than a firm conclusion.

## Historical context and scenarios

As shown above, annual net FDI declined from $9.40bn in 2024 to a preliminary $7.79bn in 2025. The first five months of 2026 remained below the prior year. Rather than invent a point forecast, companies can use three scenarios.

## Five actions for investors

1. Compare parent funding, local debt and equity.
2. Stress-test capex across multiple peso exchange rates.
3. Do not count approved projects as operating customer demand.
4. Add delay and termination conditions to site and equipment contracts.
5. Read FDI together with approvals, permits and actual construction.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Weak FDI is not a verdict against market entry</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">FDI measures funding flows, not underlying market demand. It is not a reason on its own to shelve a project that has real customer demand, supply-chain fit, talent access, or regulatory advantages. What it should do is prompt you to build slower fund execution and counterparty investment delays into your planning.</p></div>
