# Philippines Streamlines Customs for Cable Ships Under CAO 02-2026

## Introduction

Data-center competitiveness depends on more than power and land. International network resilience matters too. The Bureau of Customs has issued Customs Administrative Order No. 02-2026, clarifying temporary admission for foreign cable-laying and repair vessels. The order took effect on July 17, 2026.

## What Are Submarine Cables, and Why Do They Matter Here?

Submarine cables are fiber-optic cables laid along the seabed, and they physically carry the vast majority of international communications between countries. It might seem like international traffic runs mainly through satellites, but according to the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), submarine cables carry close to 99% of international internet traffic, with satellites handling only a small supporting share. The Philippines is no exception: nearly all everyday international communication, including email, cloud services, international calls, and data transfers for BPO (business process outsourcing) companies, travels through these cables.

Cables can be damaged by fishing-boat anchors, earthquakes, and other causes. When that happens, specialized cable-laying and repair vessels (ICLRVs) are dispatched to carry out repairs. New cable projects are also underway across the region. For example, the Apricot cable system, which connects the Philippines with Japan, Taiwan, Guam, Indonesia, and Singapore, has landed at Baler in Aurora province (Luzon) and Digos in Davao province (Mindanao). The Philippines hosts several such international cable landing stations, and foreign vessels will keep entering and leaving Philippine waters regularly for construction and maintenance work. CAO 02-2026 is the framework that governs exactly this kind of vessel entry and exit.

## What CAO 02-2026 Changes

The order covers International Cable-Laying and Repair Vessels used to survey, install, maintain, upgrade, and repair submarine cable systems. It extends to cable support for ICT and offshore energy-generation infrastructure.

Qualifying foreign vessels may receive conditional relief from import duties and taxes when used exclusively for authorized projects and re-exported after the work. Here, "temporary admission" (or temporary importation) refers to a customs mechanism that provisionally waives duties normally owed on imported goods, on condition that the goods are re-exported within a set period. It applies when equipment enters a country only for work purposes, without being sold or consumed locally — the same concept is used for items such as construction machinery or event equipment brought in temporarily. This order applies that mechanism explicitly to cable-laying and repair vessels. It also simplifies short stays and allows movement between project sites and ports without a fresh set of import documents each time.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Why the rule was needed now</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Previously, cable-laying and repair vessels calling at Philippine ports faced the same import procedures as ordinary cargo shipments each time they entered. Because the Philippines' customs jurisdictions are organized around its many ports across scattered islands, vessels moving between project sites often had to file fresh import declarations, and interpretation reportedly varied from port to port. This legal ambiguity made scheduling difficult and created compliance risk for international cable operators. CAO 02-2026 addresses this by codifying a single, consistent procedure.</p></div>

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">The practical goal is easier vessel mobilization</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Specialized cable ships are globally scarce. Unclear admission procedures can delay construction and fault repair. A written framework makes schedule and cost risks easier for operators and investors to assess.</p></div>

## Do Not Overread the Exemption

The relief primarily concerns temporary admission of the vessel. Cables, pipes, spare parts, and other imported equipment remain subject to applicable customs laws and import requirements.

The vessel must also stay within its authorized purpose and be re-exported after operations. Project teams should confirm guarantees, declarations, customs supervision, and other requirements from the source order and current port practice.

## Why It Matters to Data Centers and Investors

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Submarine-cable resilience matters inland too</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">An inland hub such as Clark still reaches international networks through domestic backbones and coastal landing stations. Subsea-route diversity and repair capability affect business continuity for cloud services, BPO, data centers, and export manufacturing.</p></div>

Investors should ask more than the advertised bandwidth:

1. Do routes connect to different submarine systems and landing stations?
2. Do domestic backbones share a physical choke point?
3. What are the repair target, failover capacity, and maintenance responsibilities?
4. Does the project separate vessel admission from materials customs planning?

CAO 02-2026 will not improve network quality by itself. It does remove one administrative bottleneck from cable construction and repair, improving predictability for digital-infrastructure investment.
