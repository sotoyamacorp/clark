# Philippines Credit Outlook Cut by Fitch and S&P: Investment Grade Intact

## Introduction

If a client or bank asks you what April's credit rating news out of the Philippines actually means for your expansion plans, would you have a clear answer?

In April 2026, both Fitch Ratings and S&P Global Ratings lowered their outlook on the Philippines. Neither agency, however, touched the underlying rating itself — both kept it squarely in investment-grade territory. "Downgrade" and "outlook cut" get conflated easily in headline coverage, but they mean different things. This article lays out exactly what changed, why it happened now, and what it plausibly means for Japanese companies' financing and planning decisions in the Philippines.

## What happened: three agencies, three calls

In April 2026, two of the three major rating agencies revised their outlook on the Philippines. Moody's held its outlook steady.

## Downgrade and outlook cut are not the same thing

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Where headlines blur the distinction</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">A "downgrade" means the rating letter grade itself moves down (say, BBB to BBB-). An "outlook" change leaves the letter grade untouched and instead revises the agency's read on where things are heading (positive, stable, or negative). What Fitch and S&P did in April was the latter — the Philippines' actual rating symbols (BBB, BBB+) did not change.</p></div>

A negative outlook is generally understood as signaling a higher-than-average probability of a downgrade over roughly the next 12 to 18 months — a shift in odds, not a confirmed outcome. In practice, plenty of sovereigns sit on a negative outlook for years without ever being downgraded.

## Why the timing?

Both Fitch and S&P pointed to the same root cause: the energy shock triggered by escalating tensions in the Middle East. As a country heavily reliant on imported oil, the Philippines feels this on two fronts — inflation and the external balance. Fitch projects the 2026 current account deficit widening to 3.8% of GDP from 3.3% in 2025, while S&P said the Middle East escalation "has increased risks for the trajectory of the country's external and fiscal metrics."

Fitch also flagged something more domestic: a stall in public investment. That shows up in the data — Philippine GDP growth slowed to 2.3% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, down from 2.8% in the first quarter, the weakest pace since 2009 outside the pandemic. The Philippine Statistics Authority attributed this to inflation from higher energy prices squeezing household spending, compounded by a sharp drop in public construction spending. First-half 2026 growth averaged just 2.6%.

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Forecasts made in April didn't match what actually happened</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">S&P's April forecast pegged 2026 growth at 5.8%. The actual second-quarter print, released in August, came in at 2.3%. Rating agency forecasts are a snapshot taken at a point in time and can diverge meaningfully from what follows — worth keeping in mind whenever you see a growth number attached to a rating action.</p></div>

Fitch also raised its inflation forecast for 2026 to 4.1%, up sharply from a below-target 1.7% in 2025.

## How the Philippine government responded

The Department of Finance (DOF) framed Fitch's decision to keep the rating itself unchanged as a reflection of "strong economic fundamentals and sound fiscal position," adding that "the Philippine economy remains on a solid footing with a robust domestic market, stable financial system, and recognized reforms." Officials were explicit that a negative outlook should be read as a cautionary signal, not a confirmed downgrade.

The central bank (BSP), reacting separately to Moody's assessment, said the banking sector "is strong, and our external buffers are solid." Fitch itself was also reported to have credited the government's active policy response — including fuel-saving measures — as a mitigating factor.

## What this means for Japanese companies

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">No need to rethink expansion plans overnight — but worth building into your financial planning</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">A sovereign rating affects more than the government's own borrowing costs — it feeds indirectly into the country risk premium that local banks and subsidiaries face when raising funds internationally. This particular move is only an outlook change, so the direct impact on borrowing costs is likely limited for now. Still, the 12-to-18-month window Fitch cited is worth tracking if you're planning peso- or dollar-denominated local financing or capital expenditure over that horizon.</p></div>

Three practical checkpoints follow from this:

- **Funding costs.** If you're weighing a loan from a local bank or a bond issuance, rating trajectory is one of the factors that can come up in rate negotiations. Worth asking your banking partner directly how they're weighting this.
- **Peso exposure.** A widening current account deficit and rising energy import costs are exactly the kind of factors that can add pressure to peso supply and demand. If your business model carries significant import costs, this is a reasonable moment to revisit your FX hedging approach.
- **Limited read-through to the investment decision itself.** The agencies themselves have been explicit that the rating level — investment grade — hasn't moved. There's little reason to delay an expansion or investment decision on the outlook change alone. What matters more in practice is tracking the underlying macro indicators behind the outlook cut — energy prices, growth data — on an ongoing basis.
