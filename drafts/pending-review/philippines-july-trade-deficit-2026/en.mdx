# Philippines' July Trade Deficit: What Companies Should Read

## Introduction

For Japanese companies entering the Philippines, trade statistics are more than macroeconomic news. What the country imports, where it sources those goods, and which products earn export revenue all inform supplier choices, customer prospects, currency exposure and logistics design.

Preliminary data released by the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) on 28 August 2026 show July exports of $8.15 billion, imports of $14.12 billion and a trade deficit of $5.97 billion. Exports increased, but imports grew faster, widening the deficit by 34.9% year on year.

That does not automatically make the wider deficit bad. Imports include raw materials and intermediate goods used in production as well as capital equipment for factories. Companies should examine the composition and map it to their own transactions instead of stopping at the headline balance.

On an unadjusted basis, exports fell from June, imports increased and the deficit widened by $1.03 billion. The PSA's seasonally adjusted series, however, shows July exports down 5.3% month on month and imports down 2.6%.

These statements are not contradictory. Unadjusted values show actual monthly transactions; seasonal adjustment removes recurring calendar patterns to illuminate the underlying direction. Procurement teams should monitor both year-on-year growth and seasonally adjusted month-on-month changes.

## The seven-month deficit reached $37.34 billion

January–July exports were $54.92 billion and imports $92.26 billion. Their difference gives a cumulative trade deficit of $37.34 billion, up roughly 29.2% from about $28.91 billion a year earlier.

The PSA said both cumulative exports and imports were the highest since the series began in 1991. Transaction volumes are expanding while imports are growing faster. The government's DBCC assumes 3% goods export growth and 5% goods import growth for 2026; the January–July data are currently running faster, although that pace need not persist through year-end.

## Electronics concentration appears on both sides

Electronics exports were $4.79 billion, or 58.8% of July exports. Manufactured goods overall were $6.61 billion, or 81.1%. Electronics were also the largest import group at $4.60 billion, or 32.6%.

Electronics is not merely an export of finished goods. Companies import components, equipment and chemicals, process or assemble them in the Philippines, and export again. Rising electronics imports can therefore support future production, but also reveal exposure to the global semiconductor cycle and customer concentration.

## About two-thirds of imports support production or investment

By broad economic category, raw materials and intermediate goods were $5.71 billion (40.4%), capital goods $3.84 billion (27.2%) and consumer goods $2.58 billion (18.3%). Raw materials, intermediate goods and capital goods together represented 67.6% of imports.

China supplied almost 30% of imports. Companies relying on Chinese components or equipment should examine maritime routes, ports, rules of origin, export controls and alternative suppliers—not price alone.

Japan ranked fourth as an export destination and third as an import origin. The simple July difference shows Philippine imports from Japan exceeding exports to Japan by about $250 million, but this says nothing about value added or intra-company trade. The implication depends on whether a Japanese company supplies equipment and inputs or buys Philippine output.

## The trade balance is not the balance of payments

The PSA trade balance is the difference between merchandise exports and imports. The BSP's balance of payments (BOP) covers external transactions more broadly, including services, income, remittances, investment, borrowing and reserve assets.

The BSP reported a $1.47 billion BOP deficit in July 2026. It differs from the $5.97 billion merchandise trade deficit because the coverage and methods differ. Worker remittances, service exports such as BPO, direct investment and portfolio flows can offset part of the merchandise gap.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h3 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Do not mix the measures</h3><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Use merchandise trade to assess sourcing, sales and logistics. Add the BOP, remittances, services trade and capital flows when assessing the peso and external liquidity.</p></div>

For a company considering Clark, the choice among Manila ports, Subic Bay and Clark International Airport directly changes landed cost. National data saying an item comes from China are only the starting point. The loading port, route frequency, customs location and inland haulage to the factory must be priced.

## Six checks companies can make now

1. Map revenue, costs and debt by yen, dollar and peso.
2. For the top ten products, record origin, alternatives, tariffs and origin rules.
3. Measure sourcing exposure to China, Japan, Korea and ASEAN by value and volume.
4. Separate normal and disruption lead times for ports, customs and inland transport.
5. Calculate the cash effect of cutting raw-material and finished-goods inventory by ten days.
6. Plot monthly PSA data against the company's orders, imports and inventories.
