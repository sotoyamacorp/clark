# Philippine Poverty Falls to 9.7%: What It Means for Consumer Markets

## Introduction

The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) announced on August 21, 2026, that the country’s population poverty rate had fallen to 9.7% in 2025. The family poverty rate was 6.4%, entering single digits for the first time.

However, a lower poverty rate does not mean the stable middle class expanded by the same amount. Japanese companies need to assess both market growth and household vulnerability.

The poverty threshold is the income needed to cover minimum food and basic non-food needs. The family and population rates differ partly because poor families can have different household sizes.

The rate fell from 23.5% in 2015 to 15.5% in 2023 and 9.7% in 2025. In the two years from 2023 to 2025 alone, the number of people in poverty declined by about 6.5 million.

## Why the rate fell

PSA reported that mean annual per-capita income rose 22.0% from 2023 to 2025, while the annual per-capita poverty threshold increased by 5.5%.

Income in the Family Income and Expenditure Survey includes wages, business income, domestic and overseas remittances, pensions, dividends, rent, and other sources. The numbers therefore reflect several channels, not employment alone.

## Lower poverty is not the same as full middle-class security

In June 2026, the World Bank reported that about 28% of Filipinos remained economically vulnerable in 2023, while only around one-quarter belonged to the economically secure middle class. Even above the poverty line, a typhoon, illness, job loss, or food-price increase can push households back below it.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Use consistent definitions in international comparisons</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">PSA’s 9.7% figure uses the Philippine national poverty line. It cannot be directly ranked against ASEAN figures based on the World Bank’s international poverty line of $4.20 per day at 2021 purchasing-power parity. A valid comparison requires the same definition and year.</p></div>

## What this means for Japanese companies

### 1. Design for segments, not one price point

Income has risen, but household financial buffers remain uneven. Consumer goods, dining, education, healthcare, finance, and e-commerce businesses should consider entry, standard, and premium price points and pack sizes.

### 2. Look beyond the national average

Income, prices, and employment structures differ across Metro Manila, the Clark area, Cebu, and provincial cities. National data shows direction, but location and hiring decisions require regional data.

### 3. Evaluate recurring income and shock resilience

Installments, subscriptions, insurance, and lending require more than a snapshot of current income. Businesses should assess job stability, remittance dependence, and capacity to pay during disasters while designing consumer protection and collections.

### 4. Employer practices can help build the market

Stable jobs, social-protection enrollment, training, and health support improve retention and strengthen local disposable income. Comparing labor costs by wages alone misses this wider effect.
