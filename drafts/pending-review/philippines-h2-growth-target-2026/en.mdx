# Government Confident on H2, World Bank Cautious: A Growth Outlook Gap

## Introduction

[Yesterday we covered the World Bank's downward revision](/en/articles/philippines-worldbank-growth-outlook-2026/) of its Philippine growth outlook, delivered in a fairly cautious tone against the backdrop of the flood-control infrastructure corruption scandal. Around the same time, though, the Philippine government struck a notably different tone. Secretary Arsenio Balisacan of the Department of Economy, Planning, and Development (DEPDev, formerly NEDA) said the second half would see "much improved" infrastructure and government spending, expressing confidence the full-year target can still be met.

Why the gap between two institutions looking at the same economy? This article covers the government's reasoning and what it means for companies operating in the Philippines.

## What Is DEPDev? A New Department, Upgraded From NEDA in 2025

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Elevated from an "authority" to a full department under Republic Act 12145</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    "DEPDev" stands for the Department of Economy, Planning, and Development. The agency long responsible for Philippine economic policy and national development planning, the National Economic and Development Authority (NEDA), was reorganized into a full-fledged department under Republic Act No. 12145 (the Economy, Planning, and Development Act), signed by President Marcos on April 10, 2025. DEPDev formally launched on May 22, 2025. Balisacan simply carried over from NEDA Secretary to DEPDev Secretary, now leading the country's economic command center with a strengthened mandate and institutional footing. For simplicity, this article refers to it as "DEPDev (formerly NEDA)."
  </p>
</div>

## Q1 Growth Was Just 2.8%; Hitting the Target Needs 3.7% Average for the Rest of the Year

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">The weakest Q1 since 2021</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    As [previously reported](/en/articles/philippines-gdp-growth-q1-2026/), the Philippines' real GDP grew just 2.8% year-on-year in Q1 2026 — the weakest first quarter since 2021. The government has progressively lowered its full-year 2026 growth target from an original 6-7% down to 3.5-4.5%. According to Secretary Balisacan, just hitting the low end of that range (3.5%) requires average growth of 3.7% across Q2 through Q4.
  </p>
</div>

---

## A Sharp Slowdown in Government Spending Is the Main Culprit: 18.7% Growth to Just 4.8%

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">"Growth would have hit 3.8% otherwise"</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    Balisacan explained that cautious budget execution in response to the [flood-control infrastructure corruption scandal](/en/articles/philippines-worldbank-growth-outlook-2026/) weighed on Q1 growth. Government spending growth slowed sharply, from 18.7% a year earlier to just 4.8% in Q1 2026. He noted that "had government spending proceeded as expected, growth would have reached 3.8%." It's worth noting the World Bank cited the same corruption scandal as a factor behind its own downward revision — both institutions are looking at the same underlying phenomenon.
  </p>
</div>

---

## Balisacan: "H2 Will Be Much Improved," Banking on Catch-Up Spending

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Normalizing budget execution, plus a favorable base effect</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    Balisacan expects the second half to see "much improved" infrastructure and government spending. Budget execution — including the stricter project verification and payment validation by the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) put in place after the corruption allegations — is gradually normalizing, and the government is pursuing a "catch-up spending" plan to fully disburse the year's budget. Economists have also pointed to a favorable base effect (last year's weaker comparison period boosting relative growth rates) as a reason to expect a second-half turnaround.
  </p>
</div>

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Specifically: a ₱1.3 trillion infrastructure program, and 9 DPWH flagship projects</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    Finance Secretary Frederick Go said the government is "determined to resume public infrastructure spending and get it back on track" in the second half. The government is targeting a ₱1.3 trillion infrastructure spending program for full-year 2026. The Department of Budget and Management (DBM) had already released ₱403.3 billion in allotments for DPWH projects as of May 2026, and nine DPWH flagship infrastructure projects worth a combined ₱210 billion are on track for completion by 2028. One concrete example: a large-scale rehabilitation of the aging Maharlika Highway (a major north-south trunk road spanning Luzon), estimated at around ₱16 billion. That said, infrastructure spending for January-May 2026 was still down 42.9% year-on-year, to ₱269.4 billion from ₱471.5 billion a year earlier — so while officials describe the program as "on track," the actual pace of second-half execution is worth watching closely.
  </p>
</div>

<p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-8">
Inflation, which had cooled to just 1.7% in 2025, is projected to jump sharply to 6.4% in 2026 — well above the BSP's 2-4% target range. The 2027 forecast of 4.5% also sits above the target's upper bound. The main drivers are the [energy price spike](/en/articles/philippines-energy-crisis-2026/) and a weaker peso. Slower growth and higher inflation occurring at the same time casts a shadow over the government's optimistic second-half scenario.
</p>

## Why This Matters for Companies

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Don't plan around the optimistic scenario alone</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    With the World Bank taking a more cautious view (3.7% for 2026) and the government expressing confidence it can defend the low end of its target range, there's a real gap between institutions. For companies operating in the Philippines, it's worth continuously monitoring how quickly the government's catch-up spending plan actually materializes, while keeping a contingency plan ready in case the optimistic scenario doesn't pan out. For businesses tied to public works or infrastructure specifically, the pace of budget-execution normalization is effectively a leading indicator of real business opportunity.
  </p>
</div>
