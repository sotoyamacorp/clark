# OECD's First Philippines Survey: Why Its Tax Incentive Proposal Matters

## Introduction

"Will PEZA and CDC tax incentives stay the same going forward?" That's not a small question for companies weighing an expansion into the Philippines. On February 12, 2026, the OECD released its first-ever Economic Survey of the Philippines, proposing a shift away from income tax holiday (ITH) style incentives toward "cost-based" incentives. That proposal touches directly on the incentive framework many Japanese companies registered with PEZA or CDC currently rely on.

This article walks through the OECD survey overall, then focuses on three areas most relevant to companies operating in or expanding into the Philippines: tax incentives, trade and investment liberalization, and fiscal risk.

## What the OECD Economic Survey Is

On February 12, 2026, the OECD released its first-ever Economic Survey of the Philippines. OECD Secretary-General Mathias Cormann and Philippine Finance Secretary Frederick Go jointly presented the findings at a press conference. The Philippines became the fourth ASEAN country, after Thailand, Indonesia, and Malaysia (and alongside Vietnam), to be covered by this OECD review.

### What an OECD Economic Survey involves

The OECD, an international organization made up mainly of developed economies, periodically publishes "Economic Surveys" evaluating both member and non-member countries' economic policies. Where IMF reviews focus mainly on macroeconomic and financial stability, OECD surveys place more weight on structural reform areas — competition policy, trade and investment, labor markets, and climate policy. This was the Philippines' first time being covered.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">The review covered March 2025 through January 2026</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    The survey covered the period from March 24, 2025 to January 30, 2026, with results published on February 12, 2026. Finance Secretary Go said the survey "aligns closely with our policy direction," adding that "the government is now, more than ever, committed to such reforms."
  </p>
</div>

### The Philippines' track record and its 2040 goal

According to the OECD's analysis, the Philippine economy has grown at over 6% annually since 2011 — outpacing the roughly 5% average of the ASEAN-4 (Thailand, Indonesia, Malaysia, Vietnam) and the under-4% average among G20 emerging markets. Under the Marcos administration, economic liberalization legislation, strengthened public-private partnerships, and modernized trade facilitation have positioned the Philippines as a leading investment destination.

At the same time, the OECD notes that reaching the government's goal of tripling per-capita income (relative to 2015) by 2040 will require average annual growth of 6% over the next decade — a target it says will become "increasingly challenging" as demographic tailwinds fade and climate risks intensify.

<div class="my-8 rounded-xl border border-navy-100 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
  <p class="mb-1 text-sm font-semibold text-navy-950">For reference: the OECD's 2026 and 2027 growth forecasts</p>
  <p class="text-xs text-navy-500 leading-relaxed">
    Around the time of the survey, the OECD was projecting 5.1% growth for 2026 and 5.8% for 2027. That said, these were medium-term projections made at the time of the survey — actual <a href="/en/articles/philippines-gdp-growth-q1-2026/" class="text-accent-700 underline hover:text-accent-500">Q1 2026 GDP growth came in at just 2.8%</a>, well below the government's own target. The OECD's forecast assumes growth accelerates in the second half of the year, so whether it plays out is worth watching in upcoming data.
  </p>
</div>

## The "ITH to Cost-Based" Incentive Proposal, Explained

For companies operating in the Philippines, the most notable part of this survey is its recommendation on how corporate tax incentives should be designed.

<div class="space-y-6 my-8">
  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">1. A proposed shift from income tax holidays to cost-based incentives</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      The OECD suggests that incentives tied to actual investment amounts or job creation ("cost-based" incentives) are more efficient and transparent for attracting investment than income tax holidays (ITH), which exempt income tax for a fixed period regardless of profitability. ITH-style incentives can end up benefiting already-profitable companies just as much as new entrants, while cost-based incentives are easier to evaluate against actual investment and employment outcomes.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">2. How this relates to PEZA and CDC's current schemes</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      Under the current PEZA framework, registered companies typically get an income tax holiday for a set period, followed by a 5% special corporate tax on gross income earned (GIE) in lieu of national and local taxes. As covered in our <a href="/en/articles/clark-create-more-tax-incentives/" class="text-accent-700 underline hover:text-accent-500">explainer on the CREATE MORE Act (RA 12066)</a>, CDC-registered companies in Clark follow the same underlying law. In other words, the OECD's proposal targets exactly this "ITH plus 5% GIE" structure — the incentive framework most existing Japanese investors actually use.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">3. No concrete legal change has been announced</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      This is an OECD policy recommendation, not a government announcement of a specific reform timeline. The CREATE MORE Act itself was only amended in 2024, in a direction that made incentives for existing investors more generous — so a major near-term overhaul seems unlikely. Still, the fact that an international body has formally raised this structural question is worth keeping in mind for medium-to-long-term planning.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

## Trade/Investment Liberalization and Fiscal Risk

Two other points from the survey are worth flagging for companies operating in the Philippines.

### Telecom and transport sector opening is already underway

The OECD credits the Philippines with easing foreign ownership restrictions in telecommunications, air/maritime transport, airports, and railways in recent years — a trend consistent with the retail-sector liberalization under the <a href="/en/articles/philippines-foreign-investment-negative-list-2026/" class="text-accent-700 underline hover:text-accent-500">13th Foreign Investment Negative List (EO 113), effective in 2026</a>. At the same time, the OECD flags that a small number of incumbents still dominate telecoms, keeping fixed broadband and mobile prices elevated compared to regional peers and the OECD average. It recommends "open access" mandates that separate network infrastructure from service provision and require incumbents to share infrastructure on regulated terms, along with strengthening the operational independence of the National Telecommunications Commission (NTC).

### Fiscal risk is also worth watching

The OECD notes that as of end-2025, national government debt reached ₱17.71 trillion, pushing the debt-to-GDP ratio to 63.2% — above the 60% threshold typically considered sustainable. It also flags that tax revenue sits at roughly 18% of GDP, well below the OECD average of over 40%, and that VAT collection efficiency captures only about 45% of potential revenue. The OECD recommends broadening the VAT base rather than raising rates, while stepping up fiscal consolidation in 2026 and maintaining infrastructure investment at around 5% of GDP.

## Why This Matters for Companies

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">If your investment payback plan assumes PEZA or CDC incentives</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    The proposed shift from ITH to cost-based incentives doesn't immediately undermine investment plans built around current incentive schemes. But the fact that an international body has formally proposed this structural change is worth registering as a 5-to-10-year policy design risk — particularly for manufacturers and other companies with long depreciation and payback horizons, who should keep an eye on how this policy discussion develops.
  </p>
</div>
