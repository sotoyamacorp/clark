# Peso Hits Record Low as BSP Pivots to Rate Hikes: What Japanese Companies Should Watch

## Introduction

When planning an expansion into the Philippines, currency and interest rate trends are easy to overlook. On July 24, 2026, the Philippine peso hit a record low of 61.847 against the US dollar — breaking its previous record of 61.75 in less than a week.

Behind this is a policy reversal: after cutting rates through 2025, the central bank (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, or BSP) has been forced to pivot toward hikes in 2026. This article walks through three connected developments — the weaker peso, persistently high inflation, and the BSP's policy shift — and what they mean for companies operating here.

## How Weak the Peso Actually Is

On July 24, 2026, the peso slid to 61.847 against the dollar, a new record low. The main driver has been rising oil prices amid renewed tension in the Middle East.

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">1. Records broken in rapid succession</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      Analysts flagged the risk of a fresh record low as early as May 2026. It played out fast: the peso broke 61.75, then broke that again days later at 61.847 — two record lows within a matter of weeks.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">

    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      Rate hikes are the standard tool for defending a currency, but commentary suggests they're having less effect than usual this time. The pressure is coming largely from an external shock (oil prices), which monetary policy alone can't fully offset.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">3. Next decision due August 27</h4>

## Why the BSP Pivoted to Hikes

    <h4 class="text-base font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">A reversal from 2025's rate cuts</h4>
    <p class="text-sm text-gray-700 mb-4">
      The BSP cut rates by a cumulative 125 basis points through 2025. In 2026, rising oil prices from Middle East tensions combined with the weakening peso have forced a pivot toward hikes instead.

    <p class="text-sm text-gray-700 mb-4">

## What This Means in Practice

**"Higher rates make the Philippines a worse place to raise capital" is too simple a takeaway.** A weaker peso and rising rates have several concrete, practical implications for companies operating here.

On the currency side, capital equipment and machinery imported from Japan may become relatively cheaper in yen terms, while ongoing yen-to-peso payments — local salaries, local sourcing — become more expensive as the peso weakens. If you're in a phase of sustained peso outflows, it may be worth evaluating forward contracts or other hedging tools.

On the rate side, local-currency borrowing costs are on an upward trend. If you're planning to raise financing through a local subsidiary, it's worth watching the rate trajectory closely when timing that decision. That said, inflation and rates shouldn't be the only lens — the Philippines' longer-term appeal (demographics, English-speaking talent, the competitiveness of its BPO industry) is a separate question from these short-term macro signals.
