# Clark's 2,367ha aviation city targets tech supply chains

## Introduction

Semiconductor, pharmaceutical and aerospace companies do not choose a site simply because it is near an airport. They evaluate power, fibre, customs, roads, suppliers and business continuity as one system.

On August 27, 2026, Clark International Airport Corporation (CIAC)—the BCDA subsidiary developing government-owned land around Clark International Airport—restated its ambition to make the 2,367-hectare Clark Aviation Capital a base for the Philippines' next technology supply chain.

In plain terms, the plan is larger than an airport-adjacent industrial estate. It brings air cargo, industrial lots, expressways, future rail, utilities and training into an aerotropolis. A master-plan zone, however, is not necessarily a serviced lot available today. Investors need to separate the long-term vision from current deliverability.

## How the 2,367 hectares are allocated

CIAC's briefer divides the estate into 1,441 hectares for Clark Airport City, 759 hectares associated with LIPAD and the airport, and 167 hectares for Clark Global City. Together they equal 2,367 hectares.

## Separate current roads from future rail

NLEX, SCTEX and TPLEX are operating corridors. SCTEX links Clark with Subic, allowing air cargo and seaborne containers to be designed as complementary modes.

The North-South Commuter Railway and the planned Subic-Clark-Manila-Batangas Cargo Railway appear as future connections. They should not be assumed in a launch-year logistics budget until delivery and operating conditions are confirmed.

Metro Manila remains deeper in headquarters, market and port functions. Subic's strength is seaport logistics. Clark's differentiator is the combination of an airport, a Freeport, aerospace and manufacturing activity, and expressway access to Central and Northern Luzon.

## Eight diligence checks for Japanese companies

1. Identify whether CIAC, CDC or a private developer controls the candidate site.
2. Inspect grading, roads, power, water, drainage and fibre lot by lot.
3. Confirm required megawatts, redundancy, outage history and backup generation.
4. Model weight, volume, temperature, dangerous-goods rules, customs and cut-off time by product.
5. Build the base case using transport available today; keep unopened rail in an upside case.
6. Separate the land lease from eligibility and registration for tax incentives.
7. Map schools, suppliers, maintenance, forwarders and customs brokers by travel time.
8. Test flood, earthquake, volcanic ash, telecom failure and alternative gateway procedures.

For low-volume, high-value and deadline-sensitive goods, airport proximity can create real value—but only after rates, schedules, customs and utility resilience are verified.</p></div>
