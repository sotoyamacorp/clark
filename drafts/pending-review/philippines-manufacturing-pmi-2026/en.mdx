# Philippines Manufacturing PMI Hits 51.8, a 5-Month High — But Supply Risks Remain

## Introduction

If you're weighing a manufacturing footprint in the Philippines, exchange rates and labor costs aren't the only inputs that matter — how busy factories actually are right now is a useful leading indicator too. S&P Global's Philippines Manufacturing PMI came in at 51.8 in July 2026, up from 50.9 in June. That's a third straight month of expansion and the strongest reading since February — a five-month high.

It's not all good news, though. While output and new orders accelerated, supply chain disruption and cost pressure — both linked to the prolonged conflict in the Middle East — got worse at the same time. This article looks past the single-month headline to the six-month trend and a regional comparison, to give a fairer read on where Philippine manufacturing actually stands.

## What Is the PMI, Exactly?

The PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index) is a monthly gauge S&P Global compiles by surveying purchasing managers at manufacturing (or services) companies across countries and regions. Respondents report whether output, new orders, employment, stocks of purchases, and suppliers' delivery times improved, held steady, or worsened compared to the previous month, and the responses are converted into a single index number.

The reading is simple to interpret: **50 is the line between expansion and contraction**. Above 50 means conditions improved from the prior month; below 50 means they worsened; exactly 50 means no change. It's important to note the PMI measures the *direction of change*, not the absolute level of output. Because it's released well ahead of official government statistics, it's widely used internationally as an early signal of where the real economy is heading.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Don't read too much into a single month</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">PMI readings can swing sharply from month to month. Rather than reacting to one good or bad print, it's worth judging the underlying direction across several consecutive months.</p></div>

## The Six-Month Trend: From February's Peak to April's Contraction and Back

The Philippines Manufacturing PMI has moved a lot in 2026. It stood at a strong 54.6 in February, slowed to 51.3 in March, then dropped into contraction at 48.3 in April — its first contraction in five months. It recovered to 50.8 in May, held at 50.9 in June, and climbed to 51.8 in July, marking three consecutive months of expansion.

According to BPI (Bank of the Philippine Islands) lead economist Emilio Neri Jr., the Philippine economy grew just 2.8% year-on-year in Q1 2026 — the weakest pace in five years. July's manufacturing pickup is being read as one early sign of the second-half recovery he's hoping for.

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Don't discount April's contraction</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">It took just two months for the index to fall from February's high into contraction in April, and the subsequent recovery in May and June barely cleared the 50 line. July's 51.8 is a real improvement, but the swings across the year are themselves a planning risk worth building into any business case.</p></div>

## What's Driving the Growth: Faster Orders and Output, With Electronics Demand in the Background

Looking at July's components, new orders grew at the fastest pace since February and outpaced their historical average. Output also grew at its strongest rate in five months, also above the long-run average. Firms increased purchasing activity to keep up with the higher order volumes.

S&P Global economist Maryam Baluch said manufacturers across the Philippines and the wider ASEAN region reported stronger demand conditions in July "following the more subdued conditions seen in the second quarter." BPI's Neri pointed to a recovery in electronics exports as one driver behind the improvement. That's consistent with trade data showing Philippine electronics exports rose 20.7% year-on-year to $26.1 billion in the first half of 2026, likely reflecting stronger AI- and data-center-related semiconductor demand feeding through into broader manufacturing order books. (See our earlier article, "<a href="/en/articles/philippines-record-exports-june-2026" class="text-accent-700 underline hover:text-accent-500">Philippines Exports Hit a Record $8.77 Billion in June</a>," for more on the export side.)

Employment told a different story: after holding flat in June, staffing levels declined moderately in July. Firms attributed this to voluntary resignations and choosing not to replace departing workers, suggesting caution about adding headcount even as demand improves. Business confidence recovered from June's low but remained "historically muted," with geopolitical uncertainty weighing on the outlook.

## Supply Chains and Cost Pressure: The Middle East Conflict Keeps Biting

The most concerning part of July's survey is supplier performance. Delivery times from suppliers lengthened at the sharpest rate since December 2024. Firms commonly attributed the delays to the ongoing war in the Middle East and its knock-on effects on supply chain health.

Cost pressures also intensified: both input and output price indexes rose above their long-run averages, and manufacturers passed a growing share of Middle East-driven cost increases through to customers. As firms bought more inputs to keep up with stronger orders and production, both finished-goods and purchased-goods inventories were drawn down, leaving stock levels lower.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Demand came back — the supply chain didn't fully reset</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Taken alone, the growth in new orders and output looks encouraging. But the same July survey shows delivery delays and cost pressure both worsening. For companies operating in — or considering — the Philippines, the improvement in demand and the deterioration in supply chain risk are happening at the same time, and shouldn't be evaluated separately.</p></div>

## How the Philippines Compares Within ASEAN

The regional ASEAN Manufacturing PMI rose to 52.8 in July from 50.5 in June — also a five-month high. S&P Global's analysis is that ASEAN manufacturing as a whole is emerging from the soft patch that persisted from March through June, driven largely by the Middle East conflict's impact on the region.

Within that regional picture, the Philippines' 51.8 sits slightly below the ASEAN average. Among the figures publicly reported, Thailand led the region at 54.2, followed by Vietnam at 52.9, with the Philippines close behind. Myanmar, at 49.3, was the only country in contraction.

The fact that the Philippines isn't leading the region is a reason not to get too optimistic when weighing an entry decision. At the same time, staying in expansion territory while ASEAN as a whole is picking up is a reasonably positive signal in context.

## What This Means for Japanese Companies

The improvement in the manufacturing PMI is a leading indicator that contract manufacturing, component supply, and factory utilization in the Philippines are trending up. If the recovery is indeed being driven partly by electronics demand, that could open opportunities for Japanese firms with strengths in inspection equipment, precision components, factory automation, and quality control.

At the same time, the deterioration in supplier delivery times and rising costs shouldn't be brushed aside. Longer lead times for domestically sourced or imported components have direct implications for production planning and just-in-time inventory strategies. If Middle East-driven cost increases persist, whether and how to pass those costs through — and whether alternative sourcing routes exist — should be part of any entry or operational decision. Rather than reading one strong month as a green light for aggressive investment, it's worth weighing the multi-month trend alongside supply chain risk.
