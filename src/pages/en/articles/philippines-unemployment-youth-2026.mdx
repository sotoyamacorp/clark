# Philippine Unemployment Hits 4.9%: Who Is in the Youth Labor Pool and What It Means for Wages

## Introduction

According to the Philippine Statistics Authority's (PSA) June 2026 Labor Force Survey, the unemployment rate rose to 4.9%, up from May's 4.8% and well above the 3.7% recorded a year earlier. This article covers what's behind the numbers, and why youth unemployment stands out in particular.

## Unemployment Rose to 4.9%, a Three-Year High

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">2.59 million people were unemployed, up sharply from 1.95 million a year earlier</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    According to the PSA's June 2026 Labor Force Survey, unemployment rose to 4.9%, up from 4.8% in May. That's a 1.2-point deterioration from the 3.7% recorded a year earlier, and the highest reading in three years. The number of unemployed reached 2.59 million (up from 2.50 million in May and 1.95 million a year earlier). Consistent with [the Q2 GDP slowdown to 2.3% we covered earlier this month](/en/articles/philippines-q2-gdp-slowdown-2026/), the broader economic slowdown appears to be showing up in the labor market as well.
  </p>
</div>

## The 592,000 Increase Is Not a Count of University Graduates

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">The youth (15-24) employment rate fell to 86.5%</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    The labor force aged 15-24 increased by 592,000 from May, while youth employment increased by 310,000. The 592,000 figure is not a count of university graduates. Under the PSA definition it can include university, vocational and senior-high graduates seeking a first job, students seeking work, training completers and people returning to active job search. The youth employment rate fell to 86.5% from 87.6% in May and 90.6% a year earlier.
  </p>
</div>

## The Labor Force Grew by 822,000 Year-on-Year, but Employment Grew by Only 184,000

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Job creation isn't keeping pace with labor supply growth</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    Looking at the labor force as a whole, June 2026 saw 822,000 more Filipinos participating in the labor market than a year earlier. But actual employment grew by only 184,000 over the same period — a clear sign that job creation hasn't kept up with the pace of labor supply growth. Total employed persons reached 50.66 million (up from 49.63 million in May and 50.47 million a year earlier), against a total labor force of 53.25 million (a labor force participation rate of 65.1%).
  </p>
</div>

## Services Account for 62.7% of Employment; Underemployment Sits at 12.1%

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">6.11 million workers say they want additional hours or another job</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    By sector, services accounted for the largest share of employment at 62.7%, followed by agriculture (20.0%) and industry (17.3%). Also worth watching: the underemployment rate (the share of people who want additional hours on top of their current job, or another job entirely) stood at 12.1% in June 2026 — 6.11 million workers. These workers don't count as "unemployed" in the headline figure, but the underemployment rate is a useful complementary gauge of real slack in the labor market.
  </p>
</div>

## Why This Matters for Companies

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Young talent is plentiful, but assessing actual hiring competitiveness takes more nuance</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    For companies considering expanding or entering the Philippines, this data cuts both ways. At the macro level, the supply of young labor is abundant — good news for hiring ease. On the other hand, the sheer number of young people who can't find work may point to a skills mismatch (a gap between what schools teach and what employers actually need), or to uneven demand by region and industry. For BPO, manufacturing, and other sectors that hire large volumes of young talent, it's worth going beyond "labor is plentiful" to assess, case by case, how competitive hiring actually is for the specific skills you need (language proficiency, technical skills, and so on).
  </p>
</div>

## Wage Impact: Entry-Level Pay May Soften, Specialist Pay May Not

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">A higher unemployment rate does not translate into across-the-board wage cuts</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">A larger applicant pool can reduce upward pressure on starting pay in general administration, retail, entry-level BPO and inexperienced production roles. But statutory minimum wages are set regionally and cannot simply fall with unemployment. Scarce capabilities—including professional English, accounting credentials, semiconductor process experience, cloud and cybersecurity—operate in a different market and may still require higher pay and retention allowances.</p>
  <p class="mt-4 text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">Employers should combine role-level salary benchmarks with training cost and attrition, rather than treating abundant youth labor as synonymous with cheap labor. A practical structure is market-median base pay for entry roles, with separate skill, certification, shift and transport allowances for hard-to-fill positions.</p>
</div>
