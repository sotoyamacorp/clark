# PEZA Approves ₱151.9B in Investments, Up 67% YoY: Sector Breakdown

## Introduction

If you're weighing an entry into the Philippines, it helps to know where foreign capital is actually flowing right now — by sector and by source country — so you can gauge where your own industry sits in that landscape.

The Philippine Economic Zone Authority (PEZA) approved ₱151.9 billion (about ₱151,901 million) in investments from January through July 2026, up 66.99% from the same period last year. But reading that headline number as "steady, accelerating growth" would be too simple. The monthly pattern is uneven — July alone fell about 40% year on year. This article breaks down the sector and country composition, checks the pace against PEZA's full-year target, and separates "approved" from "actually invested."

## ₱151.9B, 174 projects: what's behind the surge

PEZA's board approved 174 new and expansion projects from January through July 2026, up 16% from 150 a year earlier. In value terms, approvals reached ₱151.9 billion, up 66.99% from ₱90.96 billion in the same period of 2025. These projects are expected to generate $5.905 billion in exports (up 194.82% YoY) and 26,047 direct jobs nationwide.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">87% of the value came from just 25 big-ticket projects</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Of the 174 approved projects, 25 "big-ticket" projects accounted for ₱131.66 billion — roughly 87% of the total. In other words, the headline growth rate is driven far more by whether a handful of large projects show up than by broad-based growth in project count. Whether this pace holds depends less on the number of applications and more on whether large-scale projects keep coming.</p></div>

Geographically, Luzon accounted for the large majority with 141 projects, followed by the Visayas (22) and Mindanao (11) — a distribution consistent with PEZA's historical pattern of Luzon-centric investment.

## Sector breakdown: manufacturing takes nearly half

By sector, manufacturing led with 76 projects (43.7%), followed by IT-BPM (28, 16.1%), ecozone development (26, 14.9%), facilities (15, 8.6%), logistics (13, 7.5%), domestic market-oriented projects (10, 5.7%), tourism (4, 2.3%), and utilities (2, 1.1%).

Manufacturing and IT-BPM together account for nearly 60% of approvals, confirming that foreign capital still gravitates strongly toward the Philippines' export manufacturing and BPO industries. What's easy to miss is "ecozone development" ranking third — these are developer projects building out the industrial parks and ecozones themselves, arguably a leading indicator of more tenant space coming online. Logistics and facilities also carry meaningful weight, suggesting investment is broadening into the supporting industries around manufacturing supply chains, not just manufacturing itself.

## Source countries: why the Netherlands leads

The top five source countries were the Netherlands, South Korea, Singapore, Indonesia, and Germany. PEZA hasn't disclosed a value breakdown by country, but multiple monthly releases confirm the Netherlands has been a recurring top source throughout the year, even as the rest of the ranking shifts month to month.

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">"Dutch investment" doesn't necessarily mean Dutch companies</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">The Netherlands frequently tops global FDI rankings, and not just for the Philippines. One well-documented reason: multinationals commonly route capital through Dutch holding companies (so-called conduit entities) to capture favorable tax treaty terms, meaning the ultimate investor is often headquartered elsewhere. Statistics Netherlands (CBS) puts the foreign assets of these conduit companies at over €400 billion as of end-2024. That makes it risky to read the Netherlands' top ranking as proof that Dutch companies themselves are especially bullish on the Philippines.</p></div>

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">For reference: well-known companies with Dutch holding structures</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Furniture retailer IKEA is a widely cited example of a multinational anchored in the Netherlands: Inter IKEA Holding B.V., which owns the IKEA brand and intellectual property, and INGKA Holding B.V., the parent of Ingka Group (the largest IKEA retail operator), are both registered there. Google and Nike are also well-documented cases — investigative reporting (including a Bloomberg analysis and the Paradise Papers, covered by Quartz) revealed both companies used a structure known as "Double Irish with a Dutch Sandwich" to route international profits through Dutch entities during the 2010s. That said, most versions of this structure were phased out by 2020 following OECD and EU tax rule changes, so it may no longer reflect current arrangements. These are offered simply as well-known illustrations of why the Netherlands is a common holding-company jurisdiction — they have no direct connection to PEZA-approved investment.</p></div>

Japan repeatedly appears in PEZA's monthly releases as "one of the major source countries." As of end-April 2026, the top five were the Netherlands, South Korea, Indonesia, Japan, and Taiwan; for July alone it was the Netherlands, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Indonesia, and the United States. The exact January-July cumulative value and share for Japan haven't been disclosed, but Japan's presence among the top investors has been consistent.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">For reference: Japanese companies with PEZA approvals or investment announcements this period</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">PEZA's monthly releases generally don't name individual locators, but specific cases have surfaced in the press. Tokai Electronics Philippines, Inc. — the local arm of Tokai Electronics Co., Ltd., headquartered in Nagoya, Japan — signed a supplemental agreement with PEZA in July 2026 to build an additional warehouse at the Lima Technology Center Special Economic Zone in Malvar, Batangas, for storing semiconductors and electronic components. Separately, precision-components maker MinebeaMitsumi announced a ₱25 billion expansion in May 2026, timed to President Marcos's state visit to Japan, covering semiconductor back-end manufacturing, battery protection modules for data centers, and optical image stabilization components — projected to create around 3,000 jobs. MinebeaMitsumi already operates four sites in Cebu, Batangas, and Bataan as one of the country's largest Japanese investors, but we could not confirm whether this specific ₱25 billion pledge is itemized within PEZA's 174 approved projects for the period; it was announced through the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) and the presidential visit rather than a named PEZA board approval, so we present it here as a DTI/presidential-visit announcement rather than a confirmed line item in PEZA's approval count.</p></div>

## Pace toward the ₱300B target: actually running behind

PEZA closed 2025 with a final tally of ₱260.89 billion in approvals (up 21.91% from 2024) and has set a ₱300 billion target for 2026. The ₱151.9 billion approved through July represents 50.6% of that goal.

July's approvals alone came to just ₱11.21 billion across 17 projects, down roughly 40% year on year. The 67% cumulative growth rate looks strong at first glance, but it was driven mainly by large projects approved earlier in the year — recent momentum has actually cooled. Reports suggest PEZA intends to keep its full-year target for now while watching global uncertainty, with a possible review in the third quarter. The takeaway: don't read too much into any single month's number — look at the multi-month trend instead.

## Approvals and actual investment are two different things

A PEZA approval is government authorization for a company's proposed investment plan — it does not mean funds have moved. Construction, hiring, and the start of tax incentives (such as an income tax holiday) typically lag approval by several years.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">No public "realization rate" exists</h4><p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">We found no evidence that PEZA regularly publishes a statistic tracking what share of previously approved projects actually reach full operation. Given that 25 big-ticket projects account for 87% of this period's approved value, whether those specific projects proceed as planned will disproportionately determine how much of this figure ultimately materializes. For a deeper look at the gap between PEZA approvals and actual FDI as tracked by the central bank, see our related article <a href="/articles/philippines-fdi-peza-gap-2026/" class="text-accent-700 underline hover:text-accent-500">"PEZA Approvals Up 67%, Yet Actual FDI Hits a 10-Year Low"</a>.</p></div>

## What this means for Japanese companies

The sector breakdown offers a useful way to check where your own industry stands. Manufacturing and IT-BPM remain the largest categories, but that also means more established competition — many companies have already entered these spaces. Logistics, facilities, and ecozone development are smaller by count but represent the supporting infrastructure around manufacturing clusters, and demand there is likely to keep growing. If your business touches manufacturing-adjacent services, components, or logistics, these smaller categories are worth watching alongside the headline sectors.

It's also worth resisting the temptation to treat the growth headline as a signal that "now is the moment" to enter. Approvals reflect stated intent, not committed capital. Factor in the typical lag to actual operation, the month-to-month volatility, and the concentration in a handful of large projects before timing your own decision.
