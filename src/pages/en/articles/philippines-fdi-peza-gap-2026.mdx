# PEZA Approvals Up 67%, But Actual FDI Hits a 10-Year Low: What's Going On

## Introduction

"Is investment into the Philippines rising or falling?" That question doesn't have a simple answer in 2026. The Philippine Economic Zone Authority (PEZA) reported investment approvals up 67% year-on-year, while the central bank (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, or BSP) reported that actual net FDI inflows hit a 10-year low in April 2026.

These two numbers aren't really contradictory — they're measuring different things: future intent versus money that has actually moved. This article breaks down both figures and how companies should read them.

## What's Behind PEZA's 67% Approval Growth

PEZA reported ₱15.19 billion in investment approvals from January through July 2026, up 67% from ₱9.096 billion in the same period last year.

### What Is PEZA, Exactly?

The Philippine Economic Zone Authority (PEZA) is the government agency responsible for attracting and registering export-oriented businesses operating within special economic zones. It's an Investment Promotion Agency (IPA), in the same category as CDC (Clark Development Corporation) — and it's the IPA most commonly used by Japanese companies.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Main Incentives for PEZA-Registered Companies</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    The core incentives are an income tax holiday (ITH) for a set period, followed by a 5% special tax on gross income in lieu of national and local taxes, duty-free importation of capital equipment, and VAT zero-rating on qualified purchases. For more detail, see our earlier piece on the [CREATE MORE Act](/en/articles/clark-create-more-tax-incentives/) (that article focuses on CDC-registered companies, but the underlying incentive structure comes from the same law and applies to PEZA registrants too).
  </p>
</div>

<p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-8">
PEZA approvals have grown for several years straight: ₱175.7 billion in 2023, ₱214.2 billion in 2024 (+21.9%), and ₱237.1 billion in 2025 (+10.7%). 2026's first seven months alone already reached ₱151.9 billion, putting the year on pace to potentially set a new full-year record (this isn't a simple straight-line projection — treat it as a directional read on current momentum rather than a forecast).
</p>

### What Companies Are Actually Involved?

PEZA doesn't typically name individual companies in its monthly approval summaries, and the July batch of 174 projects is no exception. That said, specific company names have surfaced through PEZA's investment promotion activities in Japan.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Shin-Etsu Chemical Co., Ltd.</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    One of Japan's leading materials and electronics companies. In the Philippines, it produces and recycles rare-earth magnets used in semiconductors, EVs, and optical devices, and already employs more than 4,300 people as a PEZA-registered enterprise. During PEZA's April–May 2026 outreach in Japan, discussions covered the possibility of additional investment and expansion in the Philippines.
  </p>
</div>

<p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-8">
This suggests the current approval wave isn't driven only by new entrants — it likely includes expansion projects from Japanese companies like Shin-Etsu that already have a track record in the Philippines.
</p>

### How Big Is Japan's Share?

Monthly data from 2026 shows Japan playing a consistent role: January's approvals were reported as led mainly by investment from Japan, the Netherlands, Hong Kong, Singapore, and China. As of end-April, the top sources were the Netherlands, South Korea, Indonesia, Japan, and Taiwan; in July alone, they were the Netherlands, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Indonesia, and the US. The specific countries in the top five shift month to month, but Japan has been a recurring presence throughout the year.

That said, PEZA's public disclosures don't include an exact peso figure or percentage share for Japan's cumulative investment over January–July 2026. We can't state a precise "Japan accounts for X%" figure without that data. What is clear is that PEZA has been actively courting deeper Japanese investment — it ran multiple dedicated outreach programs in Japan between April and June 2026 — underscoring how much of a priority partner Japan is seen to be.

## What's Really Behind the 10-Year-Low FDI Figure

### What Is the BSP?

The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) is the Philippines' central bank, responsible for monetary policy, price stability, and bank supervision — the equivalent of the Bank of Japan. Beyond setting interest rates, it also compiles and publishes external-sector statistics, including FDI data, as part of the country's balance of payments reporting. The FDI figures in this article come from BSP data.

Actual net FDI inflows reported by the BSP tell a very different story.

The softer pace of domestic economic activity in early 2026 (see our earlier piece on [Q1 2026 GDP growth slowing to 2.8%](/en/articles/philippines-gdp-growth-q1-2026/)) may have also temporarily dampened expansion plans among companies already operating here.

## How to Read These Two Numbers Together

**Neither "PEZA approvals are up, so we're fine" nor "FDI is down, so this is dangerous" is the right takeaway.** These two indicators are fundamentally measuring different things.

PEZA's approval figures are a leading indicator — they reflect a company's stated intent to invest, well before capital actually moves, factories start running, or jobs are created; that typically takes years to materialize. BSP's FDI data, by contrast, is a lagging but more concrete measure of money that has actually crossed the border.

In this case, the rise in PEZA approvals suggests that the Philippines' appeal as an investment destination — driven partly by the improved tax incentive regime under the CREATE MORE Act and continued liberalization of foreign ownership rules — remains strong. The FDI decline, meanwhile, reflects near-term caution around short-term funding and additional capital deployment, including among companies already established here, amid global uncertainty. The two aren't contradictory; they're capturing different stages of the investment decision process.

The practical takeaway for Japanese companies: don't look to either figure alone for a single verdict on "is the Philippine investment climate good or bad right now." Instead, separate the question of policy and structural appeal (reflected in PEZA approval growth) from the question of near-term macro and funding conditions (reflected in FDI data). Weight the former more heavily for the underlying decision to expand, and use the latter to inform the timing of when you actually deploy capital.
