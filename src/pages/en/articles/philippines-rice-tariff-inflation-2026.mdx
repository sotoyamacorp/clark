# PH Inflation Eases to 6.2% in July, Still Elevated: The New Rice Tariff Trigger

## Introduction

Following up on [the record-low peso and BSP's pivot to rate hikes we covered in late July](/en/articles/philippines-peso-inflation-bsp-2026/), fresh data has landed on the inflation picture behind it. The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) reported July 2026 consumer price inflation of 6.2% — down slightly from June's 6.4%, but still well above the government's 2-4% target range. This article covers what's driving inflation and how the new quarterly rice tariff trigger system, introduced in 2026, actually works.

## July Inflation Was 6.2%, Down Slightly From June's 6.4%

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">The January-July average of 5% still runs well above target</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    According to the Philippine Statistics Authority's (PSA) July 2026 Consumer Price Index (2018=100), year-on-year inflation came in at 6.2%. That's down slightly from June's 6.4%, but still well above the 2-4% target range set by the government and the central bank (BSP). The January-July 2026 average also sits at 5%, confirming in the data what we flagged in [our earlier coverage of the record-low peso and BSP's shift to rate hikes](/en/articles/philippines-peso-inflation-bsp-2026/): elevated prices are proving sticky. Transport inflation eased to 11.9% (from 12.8% in June) as fuel prices cooled, acting as the main brake on the headline number.
  </p>
</div>

## Food Inflation Stayed Elevated at 5.3%, With Rice-Driven Cereals the Biggest Contributor

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Rice inflation alone has been running around 15% year-on-year</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    While transport inflation eased, food inflation held at an elevated 5.3%. Cereals and cereal products — dominated by rice — were the single largest contributor to that food inflation figure. Rice inflation alone came in at 15.0% year-on-year in June 2026 (down slightly from 15.6% in May), still a double-digit pace. Rice accounts for roughly 9% of the Philippine consumer price basket, so how rice prices move has an outsized effect on how inflation actually feels to households.
  </p>
</div>

## Actual Rice Prices Span ₱40-63/kg Depending on Type and Origin

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">The government is weighing a ₱53/kg suggested price for local rice</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    "Rice prices are rising" hides a lot of variation. As of March 2026, retail prices in Metro Manila ranged from ₱55-63/kg for imported premium rice, ₱45-49/kg for imported well-milled rice, and ₱42-45/kg for imported regular-milled rice. For local rice, premium grades ran ₱46-60/kg, well-milled averaged around ₱45/kg, and regular-milled around ₱40/kg. By July 2026, the national average retail price for regular-milled rice had climbed to ₱49.30/kg, and well-milled to ₱56.15/kg. In May 2026, the Department of Agriculture (DA) said it was considering a suggested retail price of ₱53/kg for local regular-milled rice. Because prices vary so widely by grade and origin, it's worth looking past the general "rice is expensive" narrative to see specifically which grade's cost is actually moving.
  </p>
</div>

## The Big Mac Index Says the Peso Is 53.6% Undervalued

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">PPP implies ₱27.61/$1; the actual rate is ₱59.48/$1</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    One commonly used gauge for comparing price levels across countries is The Economist's "Big Mac Index." As of January 2026, a Big Mac cost $6.12 in the US versus ₱169 in the Philippines. The implied purchasing-power-parity exchange rate from that price gap is ₱27.61 to the dollar — versus the actual exchange rate of ₱59.48, putting the peso at a 53.6% undervaluation. That's consistent with [the record-low peso levels we covered in late July](/en/articles/philippines-peso-inflation-bsp-2026/), and it points to a structural dynamic: alongside currency undervaluation comes stronger upward pressure on import prices, including for rice.
  </p>
</div>

## What the New "Rice Tariff Trigger" System Is

<div class="border-l-4 border-navy-600 bg-navy-50 p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Tariffs (15-35%) now adjust automatically each quarter based on international prices</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    Under Executive Order (EO) No. 105, signed by President Marcos, and its implementing circular (Circular No. 2025-001), the Philippines has, since January 1, 2026, run a new system that automatically adjusts rice import tariffs every quarter based on international price movements. The benchmark is the FAO (UN Food and Agriculture Organization)'s published international price for Vietnam 5% broken rice: for every 5% move in that benchmark price, the tariff rate adjusts by 5 percentage points, within a band of 15-35%. The mechanism is run by the Inter-Agency Group on Rice Tariff Adjustment (IAGRTA), which checks the international price in the final month of each quarter (March, June, September, December) and sets the rate for the following quarter.
  </p>
</div>

## Why It Was Introduced: A Pivot From a Four-Month Rice Import Ban

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Designed to balance farmer protection with consumer price stability</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    This new system was designed as a pivot away from a four-month rice import ban implemented in the second half of 2025 — an emergency measure aimed at propping up domestic prices for unmilled rice (palay) to protect local farmers. Imports resumed on January 1, 2026, but rather than simply setting a fixed tariff rate, the new mechanism works in both directions: when international prices fall, tariffs rise to protect domestic farmers' price competitiveness; when international prices rise, tariffs fall to limit pass-through to consumer prices. Within the Philippines, there's also concern that a weaker peso, layered on top of persistently high international rice prices, could compound import costs — how much this mechanism can absorb that pressure is one of the things worth watching going forward.
  </p>
</div>

## Why This Matters for Companies

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Food, retail, and food-service businesses need to track this mechanism directly</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
    For food importers, retailers, and food-service chains exposed to rice and other food input costs, this tariff trigger system is a direct driver of how predictable your input costs actually are. A quarterly-adjusting rate makes fixed annual cost projections harder — but the mechanism is also designed so that when international prices spike, the tariff cut partially offsets the increase. For food companies considering entering or expanding in the Philippines, we'd recommend going beyond the general "inflation is high/low" narrative and tracking the actual quarterly rate in practice.
  </p>
</div>
