# Philippine Bank Lending: From 12.1% in May to 9.8% in June — What Investors Should Watch

## Introduction

For companies weighing expansion into the Philippines, bank lending growth is an unglamorous but useful leading indicator — it tells you how easy it will be to raise working capital locally, and how healthy the balance sheets of your future local partners and suppliers are likely to be. The trouble is, 2026 headlines on this topic have swung from "accelerating" to "slowing" almost month to month, which makes the story hard to follow.

Here's the short version: it isn't actually a contradiction. According to the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP), year-on-year bank lending growth rose steadily from 9.3% in January 2026 to 12.1% in May — a 15-month high — before slowing sharply to 9.8% in June. Reading a single month in isolation gives you the wrong picture. This article walks through the accurate month-by-month numbers from BSP and local reporting, the split between business and consumer lending, and what it actually means for companies considering the Philippines.

The overall shape is simple: **growth accelerated for five straight months from January through May, then reversed and slowed in June.** June's 9.8% was the softest print since February's 9.6%, and BSP itself flagged that "companies and consumers are pulling back" on borrowing. July's figures were still unpublished as of mid-August 2026 — based on BSP's usual release cadence, they're likely to land in early-to-mid September.

## Why "12%" Keeps Getting Attached to the Wrong Month or the Wrong Metric

If you've seen conflicting "bank lending up 12%" headlines, there are two common sources of confusion.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Confusion #1: The "15-month high" headline is May's number</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">The "bank lending up 12.1%, fastest in 15 months" headline that ran in early July refers to <strong>May</strong> data. June's separate release (the slowdown to 9.8%) came out around July 31. Conflating the two makes it look like June also grew 12%, which it did not.</p>
</div>

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Confusion #2: U/KB lending and "whole banking system" lending are different measures</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">For April, U/KB loans outstanding (net of RRP) grew 11.4% year-on-year. Separately, a broader figure covering the entire banking system — including interbank loans and RRPs — was reported to have grown 12.3% to ₱16.67 trillion. A widely circulated "bank assets hit a record ₱29 trillion" figure that got paired with the April numbers actually reflects February data; total system assets had in fact reached ₱30.12 trillion by the end of April. When citing these figures, it matters which measure and which month you're actually looking at.</p>
</div>

Consumer lending has been driven by credit cards (up 26.6% year-on-year as of April) and auto loans. On the business side, growth has been uneven by sector: electricity/gas (+25.8% in April), transportation and storage (+24.7% in April, +24.2% in June), real estate (the single largest lending segment), and mining (+35.9% in June) have all been strong, while manufacturing loans stayed negative until turning positive in April, and construction contracted 13.9% year-on-year in June.

Lined up this way, the Philippines' 9.8% in June sits roughly in the middle of the ASEAN pack. Indonesia was actually still accelerating through June (11.51% → 12.67%), reaching its highest level in 26 months — notable because Bank Indonesia raised its policy rate to 5.75% over the same period, meaning strong credit expansion and monetary tightening are happening at the same time. Vietnam's 18.1% is even more pronounced, but this looks less like organic market growth and more like the State Bank of Vietnam's explicit growth-oriented credit policy, which has set a 15% annual target — alongside stated concern about capital flowing into real estate speculation. A high growth number here is worth discounting somewhat, since it's substantially policy-driven rather than purely demand-led.

Malaysia, by contrast, is running a notch below the Philippines in the low-5% range, which appears to reflect a calmer overall financial environment — June inflation there eased to 1.9%. Thailand is clearly the weakest in the region: its full-year 2026 banking-sector growth forecast has only just been revised up from an outright contraction to roughly +0.5%. Corporate lending is the main driver, while SME and household lending remain soft — putting Thailand's banking sector in a still-recovering position relative to its neighbors.

Japan and the US operate in fundamentally different monetary environments from ASEAN emerging markets, so these aren't apples-to-apples comparisons, but as reference points: Japan's bank lending rose 5.7% y/y in June, its highest since March 2021, with low-rate-driven credit expansion drawing attention as a factor in the Bank of Japan's policy-normalization calculus. The US, measured on a total-loans basis, was up roughly 5.9% as of the most recently available reading (Q4 2025); narrowed to commercial and industrial (C&I) loans specifically, growth was 8.0% as of June 2026 — both roughly in the same range as the Philippines' June figure (9.8%). Still, given how different policy rates, inflation, and growth stages are between emerging and developed economies, these should be read as context rather than direct benchmarks.

The overall takeaway: the Philippines' June slowdown doesn't look like Vietnam's policy-driven high-growth trajectory, nor does it look like Thailand's clear stagnation — it sits somewhere in between, best described as a gentle adjustment phase. Each country's numbers reflect its own monetary policy stance (tightening vs. easing) and growth backdrop, so rather than concluding the Philippines is simply "behind" or "ahead" of its peers, it's more useful to track each country's domestic trend on its own terms.

## What This Means for Companies Expanding into Clark

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8">
  <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">A leading indicator for local financing conditions and partner liquidity</h4>
  <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed">Companies registered with CDC and PEZA in the Clark Freeport Zone still borrow through the same nationwide network of universal and commercial banks as everyone else. National-level lending growth is a reasonable proxy for how willing local lenders currently are to extend credit, and which way the interest-rate environment is leaning. June's slowdown, together with BSP's own comment about companies and consumers pulling back, points to a modest cooling in credit appetite across the economy — useful context when assessing the financing conditions of a local partner or supplier.</p>
</div>

That said, this isn't a reason for pessimism on its own. BSP's Senior Bank Loan Officers' Survey, released July 31, found that most banks plan to keep lending standards steady or ease them further in the third quarter, and expect enterprise loan demand to hold steady or pick up. Read together, June's slowdown looks more like a temporary pause in borrower appetite than a sign that banks are tightening credit. Treating one month's deceleration as proof that "financing conditions in the Philippines are deteriorating" would be premature — the more reliable read comes from watching July's number, once published, alongside the broader quarterly and half-year trend.

<div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm my-8"><h3 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-2">Which is growing faster, consumer loans or business loans?</h3><p class="text-gray-700">Consumer lending has consistently outpaced business lending throughout 2026. As of June, consumer loans were up 17.8% year-on-year, versus 9.2% for business (production) loans.</p></div>
