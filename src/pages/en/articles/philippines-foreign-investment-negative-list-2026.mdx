# The Philippines Eases Foreign Ownership Rules: What EO 113 Means for Japanese Companies

## Introduction

"I wanted to get into retail in the Philippines, but I heard the foreign ownership rules made it too difficult." That's a reason more than a few Japanese companies have given for holding off.

That assumption changed on April 13, 2026, when President Marcos signed Executive Order No. 113. It promulgated the 13th Foreign Investment Negative List (FINL), easing foreign ownership restrictions across several sectors, retail trade included. This article breaks down what actually changed and what it means for Japanese companies.

## What is the 13th Negative List (EO 113)?

The Foreign Investment Negative List (FINL) is the official list of industries where foreign ownership is restricted or capped in the Philippines. It's issued under the Foreign Investments Act and revised periodically by the government.

On April 13, 2026, President Marcos signed Executive Order No. 113, promulgating the 13th regular FINL. It took effect on May 2, 2026, and replaces the 12th list issued back in 2022.

### What's actually restricted, in the first place

The Negative List is split into "List A" and "List B."

<div class="space-y-6 my-8">
  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">List A: reserved for Filipino nationals by the Constitution or statute</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      This covers mass media (excluding recording and internet-related businesses) and land ownership. These sectors are either entirely closed to foreign equity or capped well below majority ownership.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">List B: restricted for national security, public health, or small-business protection</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      This includes manufacturing and repair of firearms (handguns, shotguns) and ammunition, which require Philippine National Police (PNP) clearance. Most items here cap foreign ownership at 40%.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Land ownership remains restricted</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      Land ownership is, in principle, limited to Filipino citizens and Filipino-majority corporations. Foreign-owned companies typically secure land through lease agreements (up to 50 years, plus a 25-year extension) — a principle EO 113 doesn't change.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

The liberalization under EO 113 is best understood as raising the ownership cap for specific sectors, like retail, within the existing list — not a wholesale rewrite. The core of List A, including mass media and land ownership, remains untouched.

### Key changes

<div class="space-y-6 my-8">
  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">1. Foreign ownership opens up in small-scale retail</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      The outright ban on foreign equity in small retail businesses has been lifted. Foreign investors can now hold equity, provided certain capital requirements are met.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">2. Several related laws were folded in</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      The new list integrates recent liberalization laws — the Foreign Investments Act amendments (RA 11647), the Retail Trade Liberalization Act (RA 11595), and the Public Service Act amendments (RA 11659).
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="border-l-4 border-accent-500 bg-white p-6 shadow-sm">
    <h4 class="font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">3. Higher foreign ownership caps in renewable energy</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
      The renewable energy sector also saw its foreign ownership cap raised.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

## What changed for retail, in concrete numbers

Retail is where this matters most for Japanese companies.

<div class="grid grid-cols-1 md:grid-cols-2 gap-6 my-12">
  <div class="bg-white border-2 border-gray-200 rounded-lg p-6 shadow-sm">
    <div class="text-3xl mb-3">🏪</div>
    <h4 class="text-base font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Ownership cap</h4>
    <p class="text-sm text-gray-700 mb-4">
      Retail businesses with paid-up capital under ₱25 million can now take on up to 40% foreign ownership. That opens the door to a scale of retail business foreign investors previously couldn't access at all.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="bg-white border-2 border-gray-200 rounded-lg p-6 shadow-sm">
    <div class="text-3xl mb-3">💰</div>
    <h4 class="text-base font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Minimum capital requirements</h4>
    <p class="text-sm text-gray-700 mb-4">
      Under the Retail Trade Liberalization Act (RA 11595), foreign-owned retailers need at least ₱25 million in paid-up capital. If you're operating multiple stores, each one needs a minimum investment of ₱10 million.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="bg-white border-2 border-gray-200 rounded-lg p-6 shadow-sm">
    <div class="text-3xl mb-3">🔁</div>
    <h4 class="text-base font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Reciprocity requirement</h4>
    <p class="text-sm text-gray-700 mb-4">
      Foreign equity still requires reciprocity — your home country needs to allow Filipino capital to invest there too. This generally isn't an issue for Japanese investors.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="bg-white border-2 border-gray-200 rounded-lg p-6 shadow-sm">
    <div class="text-3xl mb-3">🛡️</div>
    <h4 class="text-base font-bold text-gray-900 mb-3">Sectors that remain restricted</h4>
    <p class="text-sm text-gray-700 mb-4">
      Some sectors remain restricted for reasons of national security, defense, public health, or protecting small domestic businesses (List B). Whether your industry is affected requires a case-by-case check.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

## What this means for Japanese companies

**If your company previously passed on retail or certain service-sector opportunities in the Philippines because the foreign ownership rules seemed too restrictive, this reform is worth a second look.** It particularly widens the field for companies looking to run their own branded retail operations, or invest directly without going through a local partner.

That said, the minimum capital and per-store investment requirements still represent a real bar to clear. The rules that apply depend heavily on your entry structure — direct equity, local incorporation, franchising, and so on — so we'd recommend checking the latest list alongside a qualified expert before you move forward.

### Japanese companies already established here

Japanese companies already have a track record in both retail and manufacturing. In retail, Fast Retailing (Uniqlo) entered the Philippines in 2012 and now operates one of Southeast Asia's largest flagship stores at Glorietta 5 in Makati, along with one of the region's largest logistics facilities, which opened in Cavite in 2024. In manufacturing, Toyota has operated its own plant in Santa Rosa, Laguna since 1997, and Seiko Epson has run a printer and projector manufacturing base in Lipa City, Batangas for around 20 years.

Roughly 1,500 Japanese companies are estimated to have a presence across the Philippines, most of them manufacturing and BPO operations registered through PEZA economic zones. The easing of the Negative List adds to this existing pattern by opening up new options for small and mid-sized retail and service businesses too.
